Visualization Write-Up

Write-Up

Design Rationale

How did you choose your particular visual encodings and interaction techniques? What alternatives did you consider and how did you arrive at your ultimate choices?

Diverging Color Scale

We chose a diverging color scale because the main value we want users to interpret is not simply "how many chill days are there," but whether a location is above or below the selected fruit's requirement. Warm colors represent places that fall short of the requirement, light colors represent areas near the threshold, and cool colors represent places with enough chill days. These colors are also indicative of temperature symbolism, since blue is often used to represent the cold (more chill days/positive gap) and red represents warm (less chill days/negative gap).

We initially considered coloring the map by raw chill-day counts, but that made the fruit selector less meaningful because the map would look mostly the same across fruits. Instead, we color by the gap between actual chill days and the selected fruit's chill requirement. The diverging color scale was originally going to be green to red to represent the suitability (green as valid and red as invalid), but we realized this would make the map difficult to interpret for colorblind viewers and decided to pick a scale that goes from blue to red. This means changing from a lower-chill fruit to a higher-chill fruit directly changes the map, helping users see how fruit-specific suitability changes. Moreover, when using the play feature, it becomes very clear how specific climate grid cells change in color abruptly (gap of 1 becoming -1 within a year).

Map Projection Choice

We first used d3.geoAlbersUsa() because it is commonly used for U.S. maps and gives a geographically familiar shape. However, our data comes from latitude-longitude climate grid cells, and Albers projection bent the grid in ways that made the rectangular climate cells look awkward. We switched toward a more rectangular/equirectangular-style projection because it preserves the gridded structure more clearly, which better matches the CMIP6 data format.

Grid-Cell-Level Data Instead of State-Level Aggregation

We considered aggregating the data by state because state choropleths are cleaner and easier to interpret. However, state-level aggregation hides important within-state variation, especially in large states like California, Washington, and Oregon where elevation and coastal effects matter. We ultimately kept grid-cell-level data because it better shows regional patterns and the shifting fruit belt, even though it made the map harder to implement.

We considered adding a regional drill-down feature where users could click on a state or region for more detailed information. However, this conflicted with our tooltip interaction because the visualization already supports grid-cell-level inspection on hover, and clicking on a state would make it unclear whether the user wanted state-level information or individual grid-cell information. We decided to keep the map at the grid-cell level because our project is about America's changing fruit belt, and the grid structure shows belt-like spatial patterns more clearly than state-level aggregation.

Interactive Controls and Details-on-Demand

We combined a fruit selector, scenario selector, year slider with a play button, and hover tooltips to support exploration from multiple angles. The fruit selector changes the chill-day requirement, the scenario selector lets users compare different climate futures, and the time slider/play button shows how suitability changes over the century. The tooltip provides details-on-demand for individual grid cells, so users can inspect exact chill days, requirement gaps, and suitability without overcrowding the map.

Production-Region Impact Chart

The map shows where chill is changing, but on its own, it doesn't tell you what that means for actual fruit-growing regions. So we added a chart below the map that shows chill-day trajectories for the top US production regions of the selected fruit, with one line per emissions scenario.

For the y-axis, we picked "change relative to SSP2-4.5" rather than raw chill days. This way, SSP2-4.5 (the current trajectory) sits on the zero line; the SSP1-2.6 line shows what aggressive mitigation buys; and the SSP5-8.5 line shows what inaction costs. We chose chill-day change relative to the current trajectory because the relevant question is not "how much chill is lost" but "how much chill does mitigation save versus inaction.”

The exact metric is a difference-in-differences: for each scenario, we compute the change from that scenario's 2020s decadal mean, then subtract the corresponding change under SSP2-4.5. We also smooth each line with a 10-year rolling average so year-to-year noise doesn't drown out the long-term signal.

Apple and Cherry are grown in two very different climate regions: Pacific-coastal (WA, OR, CA) and Continental-interior (NY, MI, PA, VA, UT). These regions respond differently to warming. Averaging them together would smear the signal, and dropping the Continental cells would hide about 40% of US apple acreage and the entire US tart-cherry industry. Therefore, we show two stacked subplots with a shared y-axis for Apple and Cherry, and one subplot for Pear and Plum, which are almost entirely Pacific-coastal.

Map Annotations for Production Regions

The cells that map to the top US fruit-growing regions are outlined in black so users can see at a glance which cells the chart below summarizes. We added small dots with place names (Yakima Valley, Michigan's Fruit Belt, San Joaquin Valley, etc.) so the outlines have geographic context. Switching fruits rotates which labels appear, preventing the map from getting crowded.

Data Preprocessing and Chill-Day Calculation (~1 person-hour)

We used Python to retrieve daily minimum temperature data, subset it to the continental U.S., convert temperature from Kelvin to Fahrenheit, and calculate annual chill days. This was one of the most time-consuming parts because the raw climate data was large, slow to process, and not directly usable in D3. After computing chill days, we exported a smaller CSV with latitude, longitude, year, scenario, and chill-day count. We also refined the grid resolution so the final map looked smoother while still keeping the file size manageable for browser rendering.

Initial D3 Map Implementation (~4 person-hours)

We first built a basic D3 map with climate grid cells over U.S. state boundaries. This helped us test whether the processed CSV could load correctly and whether the fruit, scenario, and year controls could update the visualization. Projection and geographic rendering took the most time. We tried several approaches, including point maps, rectangular grid cells, Voronoi-style surfaces, Albers projection, and equirectangular-style grid rendering. The main challenge was that CMIP6 data is based on latitude-longitude grid cells, not state boundaries, so making the map look clean while preserving the grid-level "fruit belt" pattern required a lot of work.

Interactive Controls and Tooltip Design (~2 person-hours)

We added the fruit selector, scenario selector, year slider, play button, hover tooltip, and current selection card. Once the data structure and map rendering were stable, these interactions were easier to implement, but we still had to make sure each interaction meaningfully changed what users saw.

Color Encoding and Legend Design (~2 person-hours)

We decided to color the map based on the gap between actual chill days and the selected fruit's chill requirement, rather than the raw number of chill days. This made the fruit selector more meaningful because the map changes based on the selected fruit.

The legend is a horizontal gradient bar with tick marks at -60, -30, 0, +30, and +60 days. The caption below updates to the selected fruit (for example, "Gap vs. Apple's 60-day chill requirement"), so it always explains what the colors mean.

Impact Chart by Production Region (~6 person-hours)

Below the map, we added a chart that tracks chill-day change over time across the top U.S. growing regions for the selected fruit, with one line per emissions scenario and SSP2-4.5 as the reference. It gives users a regional-level summary so they can compare scenarios without scanning every grid cell on the map.

Designing the metric was where most of the time went. Our first version plotted raw chill-day counts averaged across each region, which was honest but visually flat because high and low-emissions scenarios tracked each other closely. We then tried plotting each scenario as a change from its own 2020s baseline, which exposed the long-term divergence but did not make scenarios easy to compare directly (the reader had to mentally subtract one line from another). We landed on a difference of differences: for each scenario, we anchor to its own 2020s decadal mean, then subtract the SSP2-4.5 trajectory at every year. This puts SSP2-4.5 exactly on zero, so the SSP1-2.6 line directly answers "how much chill does aggressive mitigation save?" and SSP5-8.5 directly answers "how much does inaction cost?" relative to the current path.

We picked 54 top U.S. fruit-producing counties from USDA NASS data and wrote a small script that reads each county's center point, finds the nearest CMIP6 grid cell, and outputs a JSON file mapping each fruit to its sub-region and a list of cells. Those cell lists feed both the outlined cells on the map and the lines on the chart, so the chart is always summarizing exactly the cells the user can see highlighted.

We noticed that apple and cherry are grown in two very different climate areas: Pacific-coastal (WA, OR, CA) and Continental-interior (NY, MI, PA, UT). In high-emissions scenarios, Pacific cells lose chill, but many Continental cells stay the same or even gain chill, which matches the known Eastern US 'warming hole.' If we averaged these groups together, the signal would disappear. To solve this, we tagged each county by sub-region in the source CSV, and the chart shows Pacific and Continental as two stacked subplots with a shared y-axis for Apple and Cherry. Pear and Plum are mostly grown in Pacific climates, so they have one subplot.
